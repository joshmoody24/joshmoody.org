The default way to manage software state is variables. As in
"vary." As in data that changes over time.

Mutation should not be convenient. I wish software was based on
immutable, append-only values instead. I don't even want
computer science students to learn mutation until advanced
courses.

Why?

1. You can't change the past.
2. Good software models reality.
3. Mutation erases your record of the past.
4. Mutation only saves time and space.
5. Most software has time and space to spare.
6. Schools should teach what most software needs first.

Therefore, schools should teach mutation late.

For the long version, see
[Immutability Changes Everything](https://www.cidrdb.org/cidr2015/Papers/CIDR15_Paper16.pdf).

Engineering from an immutable-first perspective results in
better-designed systems. To
[paraphrase Rich Hickey](https://youtu.be/SxdOUGdseq4?si=atEsdqDpZeRNh1cg&t=2831),
there may be legitimate reasons to sacrifice immutability, but
boy, there's no reason why you shouldn't start with it.

You are probably not the exception.
[Immutability is good enough for Bigtable](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//archive/bigtable-osdi06.pdf#page=8),
so it's probably good enough for you.

And if your software manages money? Brother, cling to
immutability like your life depends on it.
[People have died.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Post_Office_scandal)

[Babylonians figured out immutability 4,000 years ago.](https://collections.carlos.emory.edu/objects/31907/cuneiform-tablet-and-envelope-with-old-babylonian-contract-f)
A Babylonian mudball is better than your database.
[So is a medieval Italian ledger.](https://gwern.net/doc/bitcoin/1938-peragallo-originandevolutionofdoubleentrybookkeeping.pdf#page=26)

Burn your facts into clay tablets.
